**Title of topic**

Micro Heat Pipes and Vapour Chambers for Smartphone and Chip Cooling

---

**5 sentence abstract**

Modern smartphone processors can generate heat faster than a sealed, fanless body under a centimetre thick can spread it, and because the user’s skin temperature rather than the silicon limit caps performance, thermal throttling can cut the frame rate of a demanding game by about a third. Micro heat pipes and vapour chambers address this passively: a working fluid evaporates at the hot spot, carries heat as latent heat through a sub-millimetre vapour core, condenses at a cooler region, and returns through micro-grooves or porous wicks by capillary pumping, which works in any orientation only because surface tension dominates gravity at these scales. This paper reviews the working principle and the recent ultra-thin vapour chamber literature, using the scaling arguments of the microscale transport course (the square–cube law, Bond and Knudsen numbers, and the Hagen–Poiseuille pressure drop). Order-of-magnitude calculations for a water-filled copper chamber show a trade-off between wick capillary pressure and permeability, and a vapour-core pressure drop that grows as the inverse cube of its thickness, so halving the core makes the chamber about eight times more resistive. The paper then discusses fabrication routes (top-down etching and bonding versus bottom-up sintered and nanostructured wicks), corrosion and fouling limits, and design strategies such as the biporous condenser-side wick, which reduced the peak-to-mean skin temperature difference by 37% in a published simulation study.
